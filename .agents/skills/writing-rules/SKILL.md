# Writing Rules for AI Agents
*The World-Building Framework for Persuasive, High-Signal Writing*

**Purpose:** Apply these rules whenever drafting anything meant to move a reader — cold outreach, pitches, landing pages, arguments, explanations, presentations, or prompts written for other AI models. These are structural and psychological rules, not grammar or syntax guidance.

**Core thesis:** Writing is not self-expression. It is applied psychology — the deliberate construction of a small, temporary world inside the reader's mind that makes an idea feel like it already belongs there. Every rule below serves one master: the effect on the reader, not the intent of the writer.

**The one rule that contains all the others:** Before writing a single sentence, identify exactly who will read this and what they already believe, want, and fear — then build every sentence that follows to fit inside that world, not yours.

---

## Core Thesis

**1. Optimize for reception, not transmission.**
The point of a piece of writing is the specific experience it produces in the reader's mind, not how accurately it reflects the writer's internal thoughts. Before drafting, ask "what will this cause the reader to feel, believe, or do?" — never "what do I want to say?"

**2. Never fabricate. Find true stories instead of inventing them.**
Persuasion built on a lie collapses the moment it's checked. The highest-leverage move available is not inventing a compelling narrative but surfacing a true fact or story the audience hasn't yet connected to their own beliefs.

## Read the Audience Before Writing a Word

**3. Identify the reader's psychological need, not just their stated need.**
The literal request ("we need a strong engineer," "we need a vendor") is the surface. The real driver underneath is usually an identity need — validation, status, belonging, conviction. Write to the need underneath the checklist, not just to the checklist.

**4. Confirm the reader's self-image; don't just make a request of them.**
The most persuasive move available is showing the reader that they — and their worldview — are correct. That turns an asymmetric ask ("please choose me") into a meeting of equals ("we already see it the same way").

**5. Speak the audience's in-group language.**
Use the specific terms a community already uses internally, not generic synonyms for them. Precise jargon signals membership faster than any explicit claim of expertise or fit.

**6. Prove the homework was done through specificity, not flattery.**
Generic praise is discounted instantly because it costs nothing to write. Naming the exact detail — the company, the product, the person, the number — is what actually signals real effort spent understanding them specifically.

**7. Let modest-sounding lines double as boasts.**
A statement that reads as self-deprecating on the surface ("I don't have hobbies outside of this") can double as direct proof of the exact trait the audience prizes most. Find the humble phrasing of the flattering trait.

## World-Building I — Fitting Into a World That Already Exists

**8. Start with atomic units.**
Open with the smallest, most obvious, non-controversial claims the reader already agrees with — statements so basic they feel unnecessary to say out loud. This is the foundation; nothing else gets built before the reader is nodding along.

**9. Layer complexity progressively.**
Never introduce the real point before the atomic units are established. Each new claim should follow as an obvious next step from the last thing the reader just agreed to, never as a leap.

**10. Structure with a zoom.**
Move top-down — whole world → region → city → single detail — or bottom-up, the reverse. Either direction works; pick whichever reaches the point in the fewest jumps.

**11. Use a frame shift to land a new idea inside a familiar one.**
Walk through the reader's existing worldview, in their own vocabulary, getting agreement at each step — then pivot the final step toward the actual idea, so it arrives as a natural extension of what they already believed rather than an import from outside.

**12. Root every claim that exceeds the reader's default belief in concrete proof.**
An unsupported claim beyond what the audience already accepts reads as noise. Attach a real case, a named comparable, or a hard analogue before asking for agreement on anything that stretches their current worldview.

**13. When no example exists yet, root the idea in a prototype instead.**
A rough, tangible version of the idea — even low-fidelity — gives the reader something to hold onto, and stands in for the case study that doesn't exist yet.

**14. Treat the first proof point as a reusable asset.**
One success becomes a citable case study that lowers the cost of every future version of the same argument. Build the first example deliberately — it keeps doing persuasive work long after it's finished.

## World-Building II — Constructing a New World

**15. Never invent from pure scratch — graft onto a world that already exists.**
A wholly novel vocabulary or mythology fails because the reader has nothing to anchor it to. Borrow a linguistic or cultural root from something already familiar, then build the original invention on top of it.

**16. Add depth through granular, "unnecessary" detail.**
The more specific and seemingly superfluous the detail — the exact mechanism, the exact tool, the exact term — the more real the world feels. Depth comes from the micro level, not from piling on more macro claims.

**17. Total internal consistency is non-negotiable.**
One unexplained contradiction breaks immersion permanently, and isn't forgiven just because the rest is strong. A rule established once must hold identically every time it recurs.

**18. Plant details early that pay off later.**
Minor, repeated references to a fact, person, or place before its full reveal make the eventual payoff feel earned rather than convenient, and quietly reinforce the rules of the world along the way.

## Cognitive Hospitality — Remove Friction

**19. Use ordinary words and short, clean sentences. Cut every needless word.**
Write so the reader's brain can run the sentence without compiling it first.

**20. Minimize decoding cost above almost everything else.**
Every unfamiliar word or clause the reader has to stop and parse is friction, and friction determines how much of the idea actually lands. Treat it as the primary enemy of comprehension — ahead of sounding sophisticated.

**21. Simplicity is a form of respect, not a concession.**
Writing simply because the material has been compressed enough to be simple is different from writing badly. Treat it as generosity toward the reader, not as dumbing anything down.

**22. Treat the title and first line as load-bearing.**
Whatever arrives first sets the interpretive frame for everything that follows. Spend disproportionate effort here relative to how short it is.

**23. Open with a concrete human stand-in for an abstract idea.**
A specific person doing a specific thing is immediately legible in a way a capability list or category description never is. Lead with the person, not the mechanism.

## The Mechanics of Persuasion

**24. Reach for one concrete story before reaching for a statistic.**
A specific instance with a beginning and an outcome outperforms an aggregate number almost every time — it's what a reader's brain actually stores and repeats to others.

**25. Vividness drives perceived truth more than frequency does — use that honestly.**
Readers judge how common or credible something is by how easily an example comes to mind, not by its real base rate. A single vivid true story can outweigh accurate statistics in a reader's gut sense of reality. Use this to choose which true story to tell — never to imply a false one.

**26. Actively counter the curse of knowledge.**
It's naturally impossible to imagine what it's like not to know what you know. For every piece of writing, explicitly ask: what does the reader already know, what's the simplest way to say this, and what context is being silently assumed?

**27. Reframe numbers into the vocabulary the reader already feels something about.**
The identical underlying fact lands differently depending on which word carries it — "salary" reads as earned and relatable, "dividend" reads as abstract and can provoke resentment. Choose the frame word on purpose.

**28. Respect "reach from ground truth" — the maximum distance a belief can move without a lie.**
A big claim that skips steps gets rejected outright. The same true claim broken into smaller sequential steps, each one just past the edge of what's already believed, gets accepted.

**29. Validate before contradicting.**
Open by agreeing with a real part of the reader's existing position before introducing something different. This lowers defensiveness and buys the attention needed to land the new idea — leading with contradiction gets an idea rejected before it's evaluated.

**30. Use dissonance deliberately, then resolve it.**
Surface two things the reader already believes that are quietly in tension. Once they feel the discomfort of holding both, the idea being pitched becomes the resolution they're motivated to accept. Always land the resolution — don't leave the tension open.

**31. Argue the single root premise, not a list of surface symptoms.**
Find the one foundational assumption the opposing position is built on and address that directly. A list of ten scattered points reads as padding and persuades no one; collapsing the one load-bearing premise takes everything built on top of it down with it.

**32. One point backed by real conviction outweighs five hedged justifications.**
When the underlying judgment is genuinely trusted, say the one true reason plainly instead of surrounding it with defensive secondary arguments. Stacking justifications signals doubt more than it signals thoroughness.

## Applying the Same Framework to Prompting AI Models

**33. Treat every prompt as spawning a bounded universe, not issuing a command.**
A vague prompt leaves a model in a broad, generic region of its own possibility space. A specific prompt collapses that space down to the narrow region the output should actually be drawn from.

**34. Specificity is the mechanism, not a stylistic preference.**
Concrete nouns, named roles, constraints, and settings pull a model's attention toward exactly the concepts that should be represented, and away from its statistically average default for that kind of request.

**35. State explicit negative constraints, not only positive goals.**
Naming a pattern to avoid (a specific tired phrasing, a structural habit) is often more effective than only describing the desired outcome, because it directly suppresses a known default behavior instead of hoping a positive instruction overrides it.

**36. Use examples as compressed law, not decoration.**
A few well-chosen examples teach a model the implicit rules of the intended output faster and more reliably than a paragraph of instructions describing those same rules in the abstract.

**37. Specify only enough to distinguish the intended world from the default — then let trained knowledge fill in the rest.**
A well-known reference point doesn't need to be re-explained in full; the model already has it. Supply the handful of specific details that steer generic knowledge toward the particular version of it that's wanted.

**38. Under-specification is the most common failure mode.**
A short, vague prompt reliably produces the flattest, most average possible output. The fix is always a more precisely constructed prompt — richer world, sharper constraints — not a different model or a retry.

## Pre-Write Checklist

Run through this before finalizing any audience-facing writing or prompt:

- [ ] Identified the reader's real psychological need, not just their surface request?
- [ ] Opening establishes common ground before making any claim?
- [ ] Using the audience's own vocabulary, not generic synonyms for it?
- [ ] Every claim that exceeds the reader's default belief backed by a concrete example, case, or prototype?
- [ ] Checked the whole piece for internal contradictions?
- [ ] Could someone with zero context follow this without stopping to decode a word or clause?
- [ ] Title or first line sets the intended frame?
- [ ] Swapped in a concrete story wherever a statistic was doing the work instead?
- [ ] Validating part of the reader's existing view before introducing a new one?
- [ ] Attacking the single root premise instead of listing surface points?
- [ ] Everything in this piece true?
- [ ] If this is a prompt for another model — is every important constraint stated explicitly, including what to avoid, rather than implied?
